Pool Plaster Finishes

At Hogan Pools, we generally recommend a pebble finish when the project and budget allow. We value its natural appearance and long-term durability. Quartz and traditional marcite are also available for homeowners looking for a more economical finish. Each involves different trade-offs in appearance, upkeep, and long-term wear, and we will walk you through them before you choose a surface.

Pebble finish Our recommendation

Small, rounded natural pebbles bound in cement, with the surface exposed so the aggregate shows. Based on our experience with how it looks and wears over time, it is the finish we recommend when it fits the project.

Strengths

  • Natural, textured appearance
  • Generally durable and resistant to everyday wear
  • Good long-term value for a pool you plan to keep

Considerations

  • Typically a higher up-front cost than quartz or marcite
  • A textured feel underfoot, which varies by product
  • Still needs balanced water and routine care

Quartz finish

Fine quartz aggregate blended into a cement-based plaster. It has a finer texture than pebble, comes in a range of colors, and is a more economical alternative.

Strengths

  • Finer texture than pebble
  • A range of color options
  • Lower cost than pebble

Considerations

  • In our experience, generally not as long-wearing as pebble
  • Color and texture can change over time with wear and water chemistry

Traditional marcite

The conventional smooth white pool plaster, made from cement and marble aggregate. It is typically the most economical of the three finishes.

Strengths

  • Classic bright, smooth appearance
  • Typically the most economical finish
  • A long track record in residential pools

Considerations

  • More sensitive to water chemistry
  • Can show staining, etching, or roughness sooner if water is not kept balanced

How any finish performs depends on the specific product, how it is applied, and how the water is cared for. Colors and textures also look different in person than on a screen, so look at actual finish samples before you decide.

Pool Plastering & Replastering Services

Pool plaster is the cementitious interior finish applied over a gunite shell. Together with the shell beneath it, it forms the pool’s watertight surface, and it shapes how the water looks and how the pool feels underfoot. A new gunite pool gets its first finish at the end of construction. Over the years, sun, temperature swings, water chemistry, and use wear that finish down, and replastering replaces it.

Preparation depends on the pool. Some surfaces need loose or failed plaster removed; others need the existing finish taken off more completely. Either way, the surface is prepared so the new finish bonds well, and any problems found underneath are addressed first. Our team is trained at Watershape University and follows National Plasterers Council (NPC) best practices, including proper fill procedures, careful start-up, and water-balance guidance.

When Does a Pool Need Replastering?

Plaster wears gradually, so the signs are easy to get used to. These are worth paying attention to:

  • A rough or sandy texture. Walls or floors that feel abrasive on feet or snag swimsuits.
  • Persistent discoloration. Stains that keep returning, or a mottled look that brushing and balanced water do not fix.
  • Etching or pitting. Small pits or a worn, eaten-away look, often from water that has been out of balance.
  • Cracks. Fine surface cracks are common and not always a concern. Wide, spreading, or numerous cracks deserve a closer look.
  • Hollow or separating plaster. Areas that sound hollow when tapped, or that chip or peel away from the shell.
  • Recurring scale. Rough, crusty mineral deposits that return soon after cleaning.

Any one of these does not automatically mean the pool needs replastering. Some stains and scale respond to cleaning and water-chemistry adjustments, while an issue that looks cosmetic can occasionally point to something deeper. A professional assessment tells the difference.

Our Replastering Process

Every pool is different, so the details of your project will vary. This is the general sequence a replastering project follows.

  1. Evaluate the pool and discuss goals

    We look at the condition of the existing surface, talk through what you have noticed, and discuss which finish fits your pool and budget.

  2. Drain and prepare safely

    The pool is drained with care, since groundwater can put pressure on an empty shell. The surface is then prepared so the new finish can bond, which may mean removing loose or failed plaster or more of the existing finish.

  3. Address necessary repairs

    Problems that preparation uncovers are taken care of before the new finish goes on.

  4. Apply the new finish

    Plaster has to be applied and finished while it is workable. An even, careful application helps the finish cure evenly for a smoother surface and more uniform color.

  5. Fill correctly

    Filling begins promptly after plastering and continues without interruption, following NPC best practices for fill procedures.

  6. Start up and balance the water

    A careful start-up protects the new surface while it cures. We give you water-balance guidance for the first weeks and beyond.

Proper Filling, Startup & Water Chemistry

The first weeks after plastering matter nearly as much as the application itself. A new cementitious finish is still curing and reacts to the water around it. Poor fill practices or unbalanced water during that time can lead to etching, scaling, staining, and uneven color that shorten the life of the finish and change how it looks.

  • Fill without stopping. Pausing a fill partway can leave a visible line in the finish.
  • Keep the water balanced. pH, alkalinity, and calcium hardness all affect how plaster cures and holds up.
  • Brush the new surface. Brushing during the early weeks helps clear plaster dust and supports an even cure.
  • Add chemicals as directed. Follow the start-up guidance for what to add and when.
  • Keep it up long term. Regular water testing and a consistent routine do more for a finish’s lifespan than almost anything else.

Start-up requirements differ by finish and product, so always follow the specific instructions you are given for your pool.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my pool need replastering, or just a cleaning?

It depends on what you are seeing. Light stains and scale can often be handled with cleaning and water-chemistry adjustments. Rough texture, pitting, hollow spots, flaking, or widespread cracking point toward replastering. A professional assessment is the surest way to know.

Which finish do you recommend?

We generally recommend a pebble finish when the project and budget allow, for its natural appearance and long-term durability. Quartz and traditional marcite are more economical alternatives, each with its own trade-offs, and we will help you weigh them for your pool.

Does the pool have to be drained?

Yes. The water has to come out so the surface can be prepared and the new finish applied. Draining an in-ground pool should be done carefully, since groundwater can put pressure on an empty shell.

How long does replastering take?

It depends on the size of the pool, the repairs needed, the finish you choose, and the weather. After we look at your pool, we can give you a clearer idea of what to expect.

How much does replastering cost?

Cost depends on the size and condition of the pool, the repairs needed, and the finish you choose, so we cannot give a fair number without seeing your pool. Contact us to talk through your project.

When can we swim again?

Not until the pool has been filled and started up according to the guidance for your finish. We will explain what to expect so the new surface gets the best start.

Will replastering fix a leak?

Not on its own. Leaks can come from plumbing, fittings, or the shell. If you suspect your pool is losing water, mention it when you reach out so it can be evaluated.

How long will new plaster last?

It depends on the finish, how consistently the water is kept balanced, how the pool is used, and the climate. Of those, water chemistry is the factor you control most.
